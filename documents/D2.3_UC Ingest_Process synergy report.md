# Ingestion and processing

Data ingestion plays a crucial role in data-centric processes, serving
as the initial step in getting data from one location to another. It is
crucial to ensure that the information is obtained and delivered
accurately and timely. The most important thing about data ingestion is
knowing what kind of information will be needed by your target
environment and understanding how that environment will use that
information once it arrives there.

Concretely, data ingestion is the process of importing and loading data
that is coming from various sources into a system. Data processing is
the method of collecting raw data and translating it into usable
information.

Both ingestion and processing as well as analysis resources are
described in various deliverables, coming from WPs 3, 4 and 5. These
deliverables contain many very detailed descriptions of the ingestion
pipelines and the processing and analysis resources and tools, separated
according to the platform system used (EOX or Rasdaman), hence they
won't be repeated here.

This is the final update on data ingestion experiences of the five UCs
and the ingestion and processing synergies between them.

In this chapter, we focus on the data inventory. Considering the overall
ambitions of FAIRiCUBE, the objective is to ingest a data set once and
make it reusable by all other use cases that require it. However, due to
the specificities of UCs requirements, and the current FAIRiCUBE Hub
setup, it proved challenging to reuse datasets without further
processing.

## FAIRiCUBE STAC Catalog

At the project\'s beginning, the four designated UCs initiated the
systematic compilation of a comprehensive list of geospatial data sets
intended to be used in their analysis. This data inventory has been
updated during the second project year and been stored on the common
shared Teams workspace and described in more detail in deliverable "D2.2
-- Report on UC data source synergies" providing detailed descriptions.
To get rid of the cumbersome way of working with an Excel file, data
requests were handled via GitHub issues within a dedicated repository
(<https://github.com/FAIRiCUBE/data-requests>). Finally, the FAIRiCUBE
STAC Catalog was created. A web-based Catalog editor has been developed
via which users can register their dataset metadata in a standardised
way (<https://catalog-editor.eoxhub.fairicube.eu/>).

One element of the common data inventory was the identification of data
sets relevant to more than one UC, and their priority. Several of the
pan-European data sets (for example, CLMS High resolution layers,
Copernicus DEM, gridded population density) are required by more than
one UC, even though not necessarily with the same priority. Climate
datasets (e.g., temperature, precipitation) and GBIF occurrence data are
required by four out of five Use Cases.

To enhance the synergies among all UCs, monthly synergy meetings (that
also include WP3 representatives to align use case data with use case
data science) are being organised. These meetings serve two primary
purposes: firstly, to discuss the overall progress and data requirements
of the UCs, and secondly, to explore the data science techniques
foreseen to be applied to the research questions specific to each UCs'
data. These collaborative meetings facilitate knowledge sharing and
foster a deeper understanding of the interconnections and potential
collaborations among the UCs.

Achieving full synergy among the UCs can be interpreted as data sets
only need to be ingested once. However, this is challenging in practice.
For instance, climate data often require different spatial or temporal
aggregations for different use cases, making a one-size-fits-all
solution impractical. Another aspect to consider is that the use cases
are still in an experimental phase. This means that requirements and
opportunities are not predefined, necessitating significant exploration
of methods and available data. This joint exploration phase led for
example to the discovery of external services that already offer climate
data via API, thus eliminating the need for ingestion.

In summary, synergies in data use often involved knowledge sharing and
development of generic tool rather than reuse of data. A valuable
outcome of the continuous exchanges between the use cases is the
discovery of useful data sources, the sharing of common challenges and
experiences, and an overall deeper understanding of the data ecosystem.

## UC experiences with data ingestion 

### UC1: Urban adaptation to climate change {#uc1-urban-adaptation-to-climate-change .list-paragraph}

To facilitate data management and processing, large EU-scale raster
datasets were registered to the SentinelHub service. The ingestion
routine for raster datasets is based on the SentinelHub documentation.
These are the steps in short:

-   raster file is tiled and converted in cloud optimized Geotiff (COG)
    locally with GDAL utility ([link to a/p
    resource](https://catalog.eoxhub.fairicube.eu/collections/no-ML%20collection/items/26YU5NATNB))

-   COG tiles are uploaded to the UC1 or to the common FAIRiCUBE object
    storage S3 bucket (configured to properly connect to SentinelHub).

```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   COG tiles are registered to SentinelHub using this Jupyter notebook
    template
    <https://github.com/FAIRiCUBE/uc1-urban-climate/blob/master/notebooks/f01_ingestion/ingestion_00_template.ipynb>.

Until now, we followed this method to ingest Urban Audit, NUTS3 (city
boundaries), Urban Atlas (land cover/land use), Environmental zones,
CLMS Tree cover density, CLMS Imperviousness.

Defining and troubleshooting the ingestion process proved to be
time-consuming, primarily due to incomplete documentation of SentinelHub
and the occasional lack of technical support. Currently, the ingestion
workflow has been fine-tuned, but there is still room for better
automation and subsequent reduction of ingestion time.

SentinelHub was initially chosen to support gridded data visualization,
too. This is a service available through their Configuration service.
However, this approach proved unsuccessful because of a bug not yet
resolved ([reported in the SentinelHub
forum](https://forum.sentinel-hub.com/t/map-bounds-in-configuration-utility-do-not-have-any-effect/9529)),
which lead us to explore other ways of visualizing gridded data.

To fulfil our requirements, vector and tabular datasets should be
ingested into a geodatabase. However, setting up a geodatabase for the
project had not been feasible. Therefore, space4environment is
self-hosting a PostGIS database server, which is also used by UC3. After
project end, the database can be migrated to NILU servers if needed.

A few large raster datasets are fortunately already freely available
through cloud services, e.g. CLMS Corine Land Cover in EuroDataCube,
ERA5 reanalysis model in Google Cloud. These datasets are accessed
through the respective host's API from the working environment.

When working at the local level, the data geospatial footprint coincides
with city boundaries. Even at the highest resolution (10m), the datasets
are small enough to be processed efficiently directly from the AWS S3
bucket.

### UC2: Biodiversity and agriculture nexus {#uc2-biodiversity-and-agriculture-nexus .list-paragraph}

For the purpose of data processing and analysis within UC2 were all
necessary datasets ingested into Rasdaman data server. These datasets
are listed and accessible on Rasdaman OGC Web Services Catalog.

In order to comply with requirement of raster coverage layers, ingested
dataset went through different adaptation/pre-processing steps mostly
related to conversion from vector data format.

1)  AgroDataCube data were acquired as geospatial vector layers with
    content information related to agricultural activities per parcel.
    Selected dataset were obtained through API and per field map content
    converted to 10 meters resolution raster layers.

2)  Climate related data consist of two thematic data collections
    related to air temperature and precipitation. From the dense time
    series we calculated monthly averages prior the ingestion.

3)  Species distribution data had undergone the process of calculation
    abundance out of species observation data originally represented as
    vector points or polygons. Obtained species abundance is represented
    as proportion of given specie per square cell unit. Ultimately,
    species distribution modelling algorithm uses regular sample points
    extracted from abundance square units together with already
    previously ingested environmental variables as an input to calculate
    distribution over entire study area as 10 meters resolution raster
    layer.

4)  Used remote sensing data from Sentinel 2 sensor were already
    available at Rasdaman. However, to use these imagery data in UC
    models we had to produce cloud free mosaics for the months Feb, Apr,
    Jun, Sep of selected spectral bands (Red, Green, Blue, NIR) and
    calculate NDVI as a derived layer.

5)  The remaining datasets (land use and objects height) were originally
    in raster data format and ready for ingestion.

All the ingested data includes records from three time steps -- years
2014, 2018 and 2022.

The data ingested into the Rasdaman data server, were essentially
intended to use for modelling of farmland birds\' distribution and
further for the causal machine learning processes. For this purpose,
functionalities from the WCPS Python Client were used to extract and
process data from the Rasdaman servers. Selected datasets could easily
be subsetted on particular study regions based on time and coordinates.
Furthermore, NDVI could be calculated by making use of the build in
*NDVI()* function, which uses satellite imagery to calculate NDVI. These
datasets can be easily downloaded and formatted to a desired extension.
Although different datasets can be in different coordinate systems, the
WCPS client also offers some functionality to transform to different
coordinate systems.

### UC3: *Drosophila* Landscape Genomics {#uc3-drosophila-landscape-genomics .list-paragraph}

Specific to UC3 we ingested pesticide layers for Europe originating from
the global pesticide grid ([Global Pesticide Grids (PEST-CHEMGRIDS),
Version 1.01 \| NASA
Earthdata](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/catalog/sedac-ciesin-sedac-fermanv1-pestg-v1.01-1.01))
in the Rasdaman infrastructure. This dataset includes 200 combinations
of crops and compounds for each dosage range of application (high and
low). In addition, demographic and socio-economic data, such as EU
Demography, are also utilized in the project. These additional earth
observation (EO) data required for landscape genomics analysis of
European *Drosophila* population data, are, however, also used by other
use cases and have already been ingested into the Rasdaman architecture.
To automatically obtain point estimates of the ingested datasets for the
coordinates and timepoints of our fruit fly collections, we developed
the program "QueryCube", which is now available as a webservice
(https://wormpicker.nilu.no/) and described in more detail in
deliverable 3.3.

We have additionally identified EO datasets relevant for research
objective that have not yet been ingested in the Rasdaman system, but
which are accessible through APIs like the Copernicus CDS API or the
Sentinel Hub at the EOX Platform. These include climate and weather
data, such as annual average temperature and precipitation, as well as
datasets related to vegetation and land use, including Corine Land
Cover, forest type, and dominant leaf type. Accordingly, we incorporated
these APIs in custom python scripts to obtain point estimates for the
coordinates and timepoints of our *Drosophila* samples. We have
additionally integrated the World Clim dataset in our analysis, which is
comprised of a set of interpolated "Biovariables" averaged across
decades of earth observations
(<https://www.worldclim.org/data/worldclim21.html>) and which can be
accessed programmatically through the R library raster.

Due to the scale and complexity of the genomic data, processed in the
use case projects, we were unable to ingest and store the entire dataset
on FAIRiCUBE services but carry out the analyses on local computer
servers. However, subsets of processed genomic data, such as allele
frequencies of 50,000 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of few
European *Drosophila* populations are ingested in the EOX Hub and can be
used to demonstrate the analytic workflow specific to UC3.

Details on data ingestion for the Citizen Science project of UC3
focusing on the city of Vienna/Austria are outlined in Section 3.2
(Synergies of Ingestion and Processing Routines, Example UC1 and UC3).

### UC4: Spatial and temporal assessment of neighbourhood building stock {#uc4-spatial-and-temporal-assessment-of-neighbourhood-building-stock .list-paragraph}

Formally, no data has been ingested during the execution of this use
case, i.e., no data ingestion routine has been used. Data was either
accessed through an API (Open Street Map data) or by manually
downloading data to local disc and later uploads to EOXHub (a very good
demonstrator of how to upload the data has been found and
documented)[^1]. Due to the storage on EOXHub, the data is accessible
for everyone within FAIRiCUBE. As a consequence, synergies may be
established by other UCs. So far, the uploaded data to the EOXHub
platform is limited to a few datasets, namely digital elevation model,
building tags from OpenStreetMap, orthoimagery, building rooftop
geometry, and tabular data. And these datasets correspond to the cities
of Barcelona, Luxemburg, Oslo, Rennes, and Vienna. Some of the
processing outcomes from UC4 (e.g., building energy performance) are in
vectorized form. Here, learning outcomes from UC1 in storing vectorized
datasets into grided format has been tested and the gridded data are
stored in a GeoTIFF format[^2].

### UC5: Validation of Phytosociological Methods through Occurrence Cubes {#uc5-validation-of-phytosociological-methods-through-occurrence-cubes .list-paragraph}

For UC5, multiple data sources were used to provide biodiversity and
environmental data. These included the Global Biodiversity Information
Facility (GBIF) for species occurrences, the European Nature Information
System (EUNIS) for the habitat map of Europe, and Copernicus and Chelsa
for topographical and climatic data.

All datasets were handled locally and downloaded either manually through
R scripts (e.g., occurrence data from GBIF) or through APIs (e.g.,
Copernicus Climate Change Service Web Catalogue), as no ingestion was
required to access the necessary data.

As stated in the previous version of this deliverable, several datasets
from the ERA5 reanalysis model were initially requested for ingestion
from this use case. However, after ingesting one dataset, we decided not
to proceed with further requests, as the data could be accessed through
API from the Copernicus Climate Change Service Web Catalogue.
Additionally, the use of ERA5 data was eventually reconsidered in favour
of Chelsa BIOCLIM data.

# Synergies of ingestion and processing routines

## Synergy example: Biodiversity data knowledge sharing (UC1, 2, 5) 

Working with biodiversity-related data typically involves obtaining
species occurrence data from relevant organizations that take care of
the collection and initial harmonisation of the information. This 'raw'
input data then is transformed into a suitable format for analysis. In
the FAIRiCUBE project, our objective is to streamline this process by
defining and producing occurrence cubes, which can be shared as data
cubes, enabling reuse both by other project partners as well as
potential users beyond FAIRiCUBE.

An occurrence cube is a gridded dataset calculated by aggregating or
distributing the available occurrences (i.e., the species field
observations, typically of presence) across a selected grid, with a
certain grid cell size and coordinate system. Space, time, and taxonomy
are evident dimensions of such hypercubes, and other, more thematic
dimensions, to be defined case by case. There may also be multiple
occurrence cubes derived from the same data, e.g., a species occurrence
cube with aggregated proportional abundance, and a species group
occurrence cube with Boolean presence values (described in Oldoni et
al.[^3]).

In FAIRiCUBE, Use Cases 1, 2 and 5 considered biodiversity related
aspects within their work, and some of the sibling projects had or have
it as their main topic (e.g., B-CUBED). Unfortunately, these all did not
start at the same time, or had different interests regarding what to
consider, e.g. plant or animal species, at which resolution (100m or
10m), which source of data to use (GBIF or national databases), and how
to process the species observations. Therefore, the synergy between the
Use Cases stayed at the level of knowledge sharing. On some occasions
more collaboration has been possible, for example when selecting models
to use for species distribution modelling, or on how to retrieve species
observation data from the GBIF platform[^4].

After several exchanges with UC2 and UC5 about occurrence cubes and
species distribution modelling, UC1 decided to implement occurrence
cubes using occurrence data from GBIF (downloaded e.g. through R APIs)
and mdata.mnhn.lu (file download) using the xarray Python library. The
occurrence locations were used to query the habitat data cube, as
demonstrated [in this processing
script](https://github.com/FAIRiCUBE/uc1-urban-climate/blob/master/processing/sdm/MaxentModel-Final.py).
The UC2, and UC5 experiences were useful to quickly gain understanding
of GBIF and their API, and to choose the optimal species distribution
model (MaxEnt) for the task of mapping invasive alien plant species in
urban areas.

UC2 has worked with the species observation data from the national NDFF
database, of which only a subset is available via GBIF. For this use
case it was needed to process at 10m grid cell resolution to be able to
find correlations between biodiversity and agriculture practices at,
farm field level scale. Besides that, the promise was, and still is,
that the NDFF data will become open data. However, unfortunately this
did not happen during the lifespan of the project. Based on the NDFF
species observation data of farmland birds, species abundance values
(instead of only occupancy) were calculated for the grid cells in the
study regions. These have then been used to derive the species richness
per grid cell, which serve as the 'outcome' variable for the causal
machine learning investigated by the use case.

And UC5 used occurrence cubes for validating phytosociological methods,
comparing EUNIS Habitat types and their main vegetation associations
with the recorded occurrence of their individual component species.

## Synergy example: possible impacts of environmental factors in cities on genetic variations of Drosophila (UC1 and UC3)

One of the central questions in climate change research in the face of
the accelerating biodiversity crisis is how organisms can cope with
changing environments in the long term. This applies particularly to
urban areas where, for example, soil sealing, increased air pollution,
and habitat fragmentation results in increased challenges to wildlife.
Moreover, urban areas are often characterized by higher ambient
temperatures compared to natural habitats, which further influences
wildlife with specific and narrow thermal niches. It is thus important
to better understand how urbanization affects the survival and
adaptation of organisms.

In this synergy project we addressed unresolved questions concerning the
evolutionary potential of organisms living in urban or natural
environments. We have compared evolutionary trajectories, i.e., changes
in allele frequencies and genetic variation, of the fruit fly
*Drosophila melanogaster,* a long-standing genetic model organism, in
urban and natural environments through space and time. The recently
extended DEST dataset (<http://dest.bio>) contains genomic data of
hundreds of fly populations from both environments that have been
collected and sequenced during the last 10 years. Many more flies\'
samples were collected and sequenced through the FAIRiCUBE citizen
science initiative Vienna City Fly in 2024.

The team at the NHM Vienna provided the allele frequency and species
distribution data which can be used to estimate genomic change due to
environmental stress. The team of space4environment provided high
resolution environmental data (land use/land cover, soil sealing, urban
tree cover and climate variables such as precipitation and temperature),
which were used to trace for critical environmental factors that may
influence genetic variation, specifically in urban areas. However, given
that *D. melanogaster* is a human commensal, we speculated that
urbanization could be even beneficial to *Drosophila* given that flies
have long adapted to co-existence with humans, which may be reflected in
higher amounts of genetic variation close to human settlements compared
to rural areas.

This synergy allowed us to optimally use domain expertise and resources
by the involved Use Cases when working on different stages of the
synergy project. Tasks which require such different domain expertise
have already been completed and are explained in the following section.

[Data selection and processing]{.underline}

The first requirement in this project was an assessment of available and
useful data for the question posed. Datasets that are potentially
relevant for the organism *Drosophila melanogaster* that specifically
cover an urban area (Vienna) and surrounding areas have been
investigated and preselected by UC3. Most datasets are provided by the
City of Vienna and offer very dense information on a variety of urban
features. The technical properties of these data like projection
systems, resolution, data format and other aspects concerning
suitability to UC1-specific workflows and FAIRiCUBE infrastructure have
been assessed and processed by UC1.

Harmonization of the available data was carried out by UC1 and
attributes relevant for the project have been newly conceptualized
together with UC3 to suit research questions centered around the
influence of urban environment on genetic variation. This was achieved
by either categorizing already existing attributes (such as daily
average temperature) as potential drivers of genetic variation or
genetic homogeneity in the population, or by recalculation and
accumulation of certain specific attributes (such as density of fruit
trees) that potentially influence the genetic variation in Viennese
*Drosophila melanogaster* populations. These attributes were categorised
based on their expected impact on the biology of fruit flies, e.g. the
presence of fruit trees (e.g. *Prunus sp*.), may have a more positive
influence on genetic diversity of *Drosophila* than the presence of
industrial buildings. We aggregated different attributes into a newly
created "Fly indicator", which can evaluate areas of interest as optimal
or non-optimal habitats for the *Drosophila melanogaster* flies, based
on the expected cumulative effect of the independent attributes on the
fly biology.

[Analysis of *Drosophila* species diversity in Vienna]{.underline}

Throughout the sampling period of the Vienna City Fly citizen science
project, 278 sampling traps were collected by citizen scientist helpers,
which resulted in a total of 19,100 sampled flies. While most of these
collections came from Vienna, additional samples were collected in other
counties of Austria as well as in Germany and Italy. Across all samples,
we identified 13 *Drosophila* species and analysed their distribution
through time and space. To facilitate similar future projects, we
developed an identification key for all observed species. By combining
abundance data with 58 bio-climatic and environmental variables, we
applied species distribution modelling with a random forest approach to
analyse habitat preferences. These analyses revealed strong differences
in putative ecological niches among the two most common species *D.
melanogaster* and *D. mercatorum* as shown in Figure 1.

We further employed redundancy analysis, which is based on a combination
of multiple regression and ordination (PCA) to reduce dimensionality.
Climatic and environmental data were used as predictor variables, while
species abundance served as the response variable. Particularly the
first ordination axis showed strong differences in the ecological
associations of the two most common *Drosophila* species, which is
consistent with the previous result. While *D. melanogaster* was
strongly associated with loose residential areas and vineyards along the
first RDA axis (Figure 2), *D. mercatorum*, which had never been
recorded in Austria prior to the Vienna City Fly project, was most
abundant in areas with large building volume indicating that the latter
species is better adopted to urban landscapes. We further observed
significant variations in seasonal occurrence and found that species
richness was negatively correlated with urbanization.

[Analysis of *Drosophila melanogaster* genetic data from Vienna City
Fly]{.underline}

We further sequenced 43 populations of *D. melanogaster* populations
from the Vienna City Fly project and performed genome-wide analyses of
genetic variation and population structure using PCA. We intersected
information of genetic variation with the above-mentioned environmental
data. PCA did not reveal significant geographic clustering of
populations within Vienna. However, we identified some outlier
populations that may be shaped by admixture with other European
populations, and which require further investigations. We did not find
any correlations between the PCA axes and any of the environmental
variables, which suggests that neither the environment, nor isolation by
distance contributes to genetic differentiation. This may suggest that
urbanity results in high genetic turnover and exchange within Vienna
driven by the high mobility and environmental fluctuations in urban
environments.

[Geospatial database and results visualization]{.underline}

For long-term storage and optimal use, it was decided to store the data
collected during the FAIRiCUBE citizen science initiative Vienna City
Fly on the PostGIS database server hosted by space4environment, in a
dedicated UC3 database. UC1 provided technical guidance on the database
schema definition and is responsible for the administration of the UC3
database.

The infrastructure and knowledge sharing between UC1 and UC3 also
concerned the data visualization aspect. In fact, UC3 decided to adopt
the data visualization tool Grafana to present their results. The tool
was adopted earlier by UC1 (for their cities clustering analysis task),
which helped in setting up the tool on the EOXHub infrastructure and
provided support in designing the dashboards.

# Conclusions and outlook

While not all objectives related to workflow setup and implementation
have been fully achieved, there has been promising progress in the area
of ingestion and processing synergies. Regarding data ingestion, the
procedures for ingesting data into the EOX and rasdaman systems appear
to be well-defined. However, there is a lack of clear interfaces for
direct interaction. It is necessary to develop and implement
improvements that enable the direct retrieval of ingested data,
irrespective of the system in which it was ingested.

On the processing and analytical side, the synergies regarding
occurrence cubes are an interesting playing field for technological
development of a common processing of dedicated cubes (how are they
created, i.e., processed), but also at a later stage for the conception
of synergetic analytical tools or machine learning applications.

Another interesting synergy has developed with the collaboration of UC1
and UC3 on a project that UC3 has conceived for Vienna. The aim of this
new project is to create a network of fly collectors by which flies
should be collected to sequence their genomic variations and relate them
to environmental conditions. Both during the process of identifying
ideal places to position fly traps (the "Fly indicator") and later
during the analytical phase, UC1 provides urban data on various
environmental topics.

Another synergy is related to the application of urban areas as
reference units for the analysis. Both UC1 and UC4 work on cities, but
with a different focus. Nevertheless, we have merged the two UCs during
the preparation of the first ever FAIRiCUBE UC Seminar that is part of
the stakeholder consultation process, organized by WP6. But so far,
there are only very limited processing synergies, so there is a lot of
room for further exploration.

Compared to the first version of the report, the UCs have made progress
regarding the ingestion of data sets, also further synergies could be
identified. It will nevertheless be important to deepen those further
which will now happen during the coming months.
